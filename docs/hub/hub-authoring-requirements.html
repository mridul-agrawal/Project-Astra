Project Astra · Gurukul Hub

Hub Authoring Requirements

What building and maintaining the Gurukul Hub must feel like from the designer's chair. This describes the experience, not the tooling — the architecture comes next, and is deliberately not decided here.

The bar: a designer who has never opened the codebase can take the Gurukul Hub, build it, populate it, configure it, change it, preview any visit, and test any part of it — without asking a programmer.

What the Hub is

Stated plainly, because several requirements only make sense against it.

  • Free-roam, not tile-based. She walks in continuous space. No grid movement, no per-tile gameplay, no Tilemap anywhere in the authoring model.
  • One large painted map, with props on top. Pixel art at 32 px to the world unit. Some props are animated; an animated prop is placed exactly like a still one.
  • Four visits over largely the same space. What differs between them is the cast and where they stand, what each says, which doors are open, which objectives run, and which scripted moments fire. The ground does not move.
  • One place on screen at a time — the Gurukul exterior, plus the student-house interior, the Guru's Quarters and the Library.

Five principles

  1. The scene is the truth for where. A dedicated surface is the truth for what it means. Position, size, facing, extent and reach are dragged in the scene, never typed. Which conversation, which objective, which gate, which visit — edited in a real interface, never hunted for across loose asset files.
  2. A visit is a lens, not a copy. The designer picks “Visit 2” and the scene becomes Visit 2 — that cast, in those positions, with those doors locked. Dragging a character in that mode edits Visit 2's data, not the scene. There is never a second copy of the Gurukul to keep in sync.
  3. Nothing that can be derived is ever typed. No ids, file paths, layer names, import numbers, sorting orders or cross-references typed by hand. If two things must match, the designer picks one from a list of the other.
  4. Prevent first, detect second, report last. The best error message is the one that could not happen. Lists instead of text fields. Palettes instead of raw assets. Automatic layers and collision. Validation is the backstop, not the mechanism.
  5. Anything can be tested in seconds. No change should require playing from the beginning to see. Any conversation, any objective, any door, any visit — from where the designer is standing.

Part one · The designer's day

Seven journeys. Each is a promise about how the work should feel.

J1Starting the Gurukul

Opens the Hub scene. The Gurukul is there — the painted map, the trees, the houses, the river — visible, selectable, editable. Not an empty transform. Not something that appears only on Play.

Drops the artist's folder of art into the project. The tool takes it from there: correct pixel settings, correct scale, ready to use. The designer never opens an import inspector.

Drags the painted map into place. It lands at the right size and the right depth, first try.

J2Placing decoration

Wants twenty trees along the north edge. Opens a palette of hub objects — thumbnails, searchable, grouped. Picks Tree. Clicks twenty times. Each lands on a whole pixel, sorts so she walks behind it, and blocks at its trunk — without the designer thinking about any of that.

One is wrong. Selects it, drags it, deletes it, duplicates it, flips it. Normal handles, normal shortcuts, normal undo.

Nothing about this asks whether a tree is a prefab.

J3Making something interactable

Wants a noticeboard she can read. Picks Noticeboard from the palette — it arrives already interactable. A panel shows what it needs: what she sees, what happens, and when it is available.

Picks the conversation from a list of real conversations. If the right one does not exist yet, creates it from that same list without leaving the panel.

In the scene the noticeboard now draws a soft circle showing where she must stand, and a label showing the verb. Decoration draws nothing. At a glance, the designer sees what in the room is alive.

J4Setting up Visit 2

Switches the scene's visit to Hub Visit 2. The room repopulates: the cast stands where Visit 2 puts them, the Library door shows a lock, the objective panel lists Visit 2's chain.

Drags Kaal from the courtyard into the Library. That writes Visit 2's placement. Visit 1 is untouched, and the interface makes it obvious this is a per-visit change rather than a change to the world.

A “what is different here” view lists everything Visit 2 changes relative to Visit 1 — so a visit is reasoned about as a delta, not re-read from scratch.

J5Writing a conversation and hooking it up

Needs Madhavi to say something new. Writes it in a conversation editor — speaker, lines, choices, branches — without touching an asset file or knowing where it is stored.

Assigns it to Madhavi for Visit 2 by picking her, picking the visit, picking the conversation. Three choices from lists. No ids.

Presses one button and hears it: the game starts with Madhavi in front of her, mid-Visit-2.

J6A door locked behind an objective

Places the Library door. Sets Locked until → picks Learn Weapon Theory from that visit's objectives. Sets What she says when it's locked → picks or writes the line.

The door now draws a lock in the scene while that visit is selected. Selecting the objective highlights everything depending on it.

At no point does the designer invent a flag name, or remember one.

J7Something is wrong

Presses Play and the prompt will not appear on the noticeboard. A live panel answers directly: “Noticeboard — not offered: she is facing away.” Not a stack trace, not silence. The three or four real reasons an interaction does not fire, named in the designer's language.

Separately, a problems list is always available: an objective nothing can complete, a marker over someone not in that visit, a door leading nowhere, a character inside a wall. Each row selects the thing at fault. Where a fix is unambiguous, a button applies it.

Part two · Requirements

01What the designer must be able to do

ComposePlace, move, rotate, flip, scale, duplicate, group and delete any environmental object, directly in the scene
ImportBring in the artist's art and use it immediately, including animated props
Build placesCreate a room, set its extent, and connect it to others by doors
Make things liveTurn an object into something she can talk to, read, enter or use — and back again
CastCreate characters, place them, and give them something to say
Vary by visitChange placements, conversations, availability and world state per visit
Author progressionCreate objectives, order them, define what completes them and what they change
Script momentsAuthor sequences — someone walks in, says a line, the camera moves, a door opens
PreviewSee any visit, at any point in its progression, without playing to it
TestTrigger any single conversation, objective, door or event on demand
DiagnoseFind out why something is not behaving, in plain language
IterateDo all of the above repeatedly and quickly, without a programmer

02What the designer must never do by hand

  • Open a texture import inspector, or know what Pixels Per Unit is
  • Set a layer, a sorting layer, or a sorting order
  • Add a collider, or guess its size
  • Think about depth so she walks behind a tree
  • Type an id that has to match an id somewhere else
  • Know that ScriptableObjects exist, or where any asset file lives
  • Create an asset purely so something else can point at it
  • Keep two lists in agreement
  • Decide whether something should be a prefab
  • Nudge an object to sit on a whole pixel
  • Register a new room, visit or quest anywhere
  • Restart the game to see a change
  • Read or edit anything resembling code or serialised data

03What the designer must be able to see

Always, in the scene

  • The actual environment, at edit time, without pressing Play
  • The room's extent — where the camera stops and she cannot walk
  • What blocks, drawn clearly, distinct from decoration
  • Which objects are interactive, with their verb and reach
  • Where the player starts

While playing

  • Current visit, active objective, progress
  • What is in reach, and what a press would do
  • For anything not offered: why not

When a visit is selected

  • That visit's cast, in that visit's positions
  • Which doors are locked, and by what
  • Which props are in a non-default state
  • That visit's objective chain, with the active one marked
  • What differs from the previous visit

Always, somewhere

  • Everything currently wrong, each row clickable
  • Where any conversation, objective or prop is used

04What must be visual and direct

Anything spatial. Without exception — position, rotation, flip, scale, grouping, room extent, what blocks and its shape, where a door is and which way she faces it, where she spawns, where a character stands in a given visit, how far an interaction reaches, and the route a scripted walk takes.

All by dragging, with handles, with live feedback, with undo.

05What must be automatic

  • Art import settings, on drop
  • Placement on whole pixels
  • Depth sorting, from an object's base
  • Layers and collision on anything placed from the palette
  • Identifiers, generated from the name the designer typed
  • Where new assets are created, and their registration in whatever indexes them
  • Scene organisation — placed things land somewhere sensible and stay tidy
  • Keeping a room's camera bounds in step with its art
  • Reverse links: if A points at B, B knows about A without anyone maintaining it

06What belongs in the scene

The world. Everything in 04, plus: selecting an object shows its gameplay configuration there, without hunting for a matching asset.

07What belongs in a dedicated interface

The relationships. These are lists, tables and graphs; a scene is a bad place for them.

  • The visits, and what each changes
  • The objective chain per visit, with its conditions and effects
  • Conversations, their branches, and who says what when
  • Scripted sequences, as ordered steps
  • Gates, and what opens them
  • The problems list, and search across everything

The two must be linked in both directions. Selecting in the scene reveals the row; selecting the row frames and highlights the object. Neither surface is ever a dead end.

08How content is created

  • New content is created from the place where it is needed. Assigning a conversation that does not exist offers to create it, right there, and assigns it.
  • Creation asks only what a human must decide — a name, and a choice or two. The rest is filled in.
  • A new room, visit or quest starts from a working template, not from nothing.
  • New content is never created by a process that could overwrite existing content.

09How content is modified

  • Everything is editable in place; nothing needs deleting and re-making.
  • Renaming is safe. References follow.
  • Deleting warns if anything points at the thing being deleted, and says what.
  • Undo works for every operation, including data edits, not only scene ones.
  • Multi-select and edit-many for the repetitive cases.
  • Nothing the designer authored is ever regenerated or overwritten by a tool.

10How reusable assets work

  • Objects used in more than one place are reusable, and editing the original updates every copy.
  • The designer never decides this. The tool offers to make something reusable when it notices repetition, and handles the conversion.
  • One-off objects stay one-offs. There is no ceremony for placing a single rock.
  • A reusable object carries its behaviour with it — a bench that blocks, blocks everywhere.
  • Reusable objects live in a browsable, searchable, thumbnailed palette, grouped by kind, with recently-used to hand.

11How per-visit variation works

This is the part most likely to become unmanageable, so it gets the strongest requirements.

  • The visit is a mode the designer is in, chosen once and visible at all times. Everything shown and everything edited is with respect to that visit.
  • Per-visit edits are visibly per-visit — the designer can always tell whether they are changing the world or this visit's version of it.
  • A visit is authored as a difference from the one before, and can be read that way: “Visit 3 moves Kaal to the river, unlocks the Library, and swaps three conversations.”
  • Creating the next visit starts from a copy of the previous one.
  • Any visit can be previewed at any point in its progression, not only at its start.
  • Content shared by every visit is edited once and cannot be accidentally forked.

12How interaction and behaviour are authored

  • Turning a decoration into something interactive is a single action, reversible.
  • What it does is chosen from a small, plain vocabulary — talk, read, enter, leave, use — not from a type hierarchy.
  • Its condition and its result are chosen from lists of real things: this conversation, this objective, this room.
  • The same applies to scripted moments: an ordered list of plain steps — walk here, say this, wait, open that, move the camera — with the walk route draggable in the scene.
  • Adding a new kind of interaction is a programmer's job. Using every existing kind is not.

13How validation and prevention work

Prevented outright

  • Anything chosen from a list cannot be spelled wrong
  • Anything placed from the palette cannot be missing its setup
  • A door cannot point at a room that does not exist
  • An objective cannot be completed by something not on its list

Detected and shown

  • An objective nothing can finish, or with no next step
  • A marked target absent from that visit
  • A character or spawn inside something solid
  • Somewhere she cannot reach, or that takes too long to walk to
  • A conversation referenced but never authored, or authored but never used
  • A gate that can never open

How it is shown

  • Live, not on demand — a persistent count, and a badge in the scene on the offending object
  • Every row selects the thing at fault
  • A fix button where the fix is unambiguous
  • Severity separated: broken versus worth a look

14How previewing and testing work

  • Preview without playing. Choosing a visit shows it immediately in the editor.
  • Play from here. Start at this visit, at this point in its progression, with her standing where the designer is looking.
  • Play this one thing. Select a conversation, objective, door or event, and run just it.
  • Jump within a visit. Move to any objective in the chain without completing the earlier ones.
  • Return to where you were. Leaving play does not lose the editing context.

Everything above is reachable in one action from where the designer already is.

15How debugging works

From the designer's chair, debugging is one question: why is this not doing what I expect? The tools must answer it directly.

  • A live panel during play: current visit, active objective and progress, what is in reach, and what a press would do.
  • The “why not” answer. When something is not offered, the reason is named: facing away, too far, gate shut, nothing to say, already used, another thing has priority.
  • A running log in designer language: “Talked to Kaal · objective 1 → 2/5”.
  • The ability to force a state — open this gate, complete this objective, raise this flag — to reach the case being tested.
  • No part of this requires reading a console.

17How this scales

Assume it gets four times bigger: a dozen visits, ten rooms, hundreds of props and conversations.

  • Every list is searchable and filterable. Nothing relies on scrolling.
  • The palette is categorised, with favourites and recents.
  • The scene stays navigable: rooms are isolated for editing, and the hierarchy is kept organised without being asked.
  • Visits are read as differences, so understanding Visit 9 does not mean reading Visits 1–8.
  • Validation stays fast enough to run continuously as content grows.
  • “Where is this used?” works for every piece of content, and stays instant.
  • Two people can work on different rooms without colliding.
  • Bulk operations exist for the cases that would otherwise be a hundred manual edits.

18Quality of life

What separates a tool that works from one that feels professional.

  • Undo everywhere, including data edits, never half-applied
  • Copy and paste a configured object between rooms, configuration intact
  • Duplicate anything — an object, a visit, an objective, a conversation — as a starting point
  • Search everything from one box
  • Find usages of any conversation, objective, character or prop
  • Pixel snapping on by default, because this is pixel art and half-pixels shimmer
  • Keyboard shortcuts for the palette and for switching visits
  • Toggleable overlays — blocking, reach, markers, spawns — so the view can be made clean for judging how the place looks
  • Non-destructive always. No tool ever regenerates over hand-authored work
  • Clear state at all times — which room, which visit, what is selected, what is unsaved
  • Sensible defaults, so placing something and doing nothing else still works
  • Recoverable mistakes — nothing possible in these tools should be unrecoverable

Non-goals

Named so the tooling does not drift into them.

  • Not a general level editor. It serves the Gurukul Hub.
  • Not an art tool. Art is made elsewhere and imported.
  • Not a replacement for Unity's scene view — it builds on it.
  • Not a code-authoring surface. New kinds of behaviour remain a programmer's job.
  • Not tile-based. Nothing in this workflow uses a grid or a Tilemap.
  • It does not author the Gurukul map. The designer does that, with these tools.

How we will know it worked

Tested by the designer, not by the programmer.

  1. A designer opens the Hub scene and sees the Gurukul, without pressing Play.
  2. They build a corner of it — ground, trees, a house, a path — without asking what a prefab is.
  3. They add a readable noticeboard and hear it in the game inside a minute.
  4. They set up Visit 2 differently from Visit 1 and flip between the two to compare.
  5. They deliberately break something and the tools say what and where before Play is pressed.
  6. They test one conversation, mid-Visit-3, without playing anything before it.
  7. They do all of it without opening a script, an asset file, or the console.
  8. They can explain the workflow to somebody else in five minutes.

Next: design the tooling architecture that satisfies this — how much lives in the scene, how much in dedicated windows, what is one tool and what is several, and the phased plan to build it.
